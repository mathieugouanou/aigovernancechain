AI chatbot liability: in Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149 (14 February 2024), a Canadian tribunal held Air Canada responsible for misleading bereavement-fare information from its website chatbot and ordered it to pay CAD 812.02. The tribunal said it makes no difference whether information comes from a static page or a chatbot.

0:18What the chatbot said

A passenger's grandmother has just died. The passenger asks Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares.

The chatbot says the reduced rate can be claimed within 90 days of the ticket being issued. So the passenger flies, then applies.

0:34The refusal and the ruling

The airline refuses. Its policy page said bereavement rates don't cover travel already completed.

In effect, said the tribunal, the airline treated its chatbot as a separate legal entity. A remarkable submission. Its finding: it makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot.

In February 2024, the airline was ordered to pay just over 812 Canadian dollars. Small money. Big principle.

Short answers

Is a company liable for what its chatbot says?

In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149), a Canadian tribunal held the airline responsible for its chatbot's misleading answer and found negligent misrepresentation. Rules differ by country. Not legal advice.

What did the Air Canada chatbot say?

That a reduced bereavement rate could be claimed within 90 days of the ticket being issued. The airline's policy page said bereavement rates did not apply after travel.

How do you stop a chatbot from making promises?

Answer policy questions only from current policy text with the source linked, send promises, refunds and discounts to a human or a hard rule, and log every answer.
